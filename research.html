Research. Population health at the intersection of climate, geography, and inequality.

Programme of work

My research examines how environmental exposures, geographic context, and social inequality jointly shape population health outcomes — particularly maternal, perinatal, and child health in sub-Saharan Africa. The work is organised around three connected themes. The first asks how climate variability, and extreme heat in particular, affects demographic outcomes for pregnant women and infants. The second examines how ethnic and geographic stratification produces inequalities in survival and health-service access, and how these inequalities are concealed or revealed by the way population statistics are aggregated. The third develops the spatial and computational infrastructure required to bring environmental, conflict, and survey data into a single analytical frame.

Across all three themes I draw on the Demographic and Health Surveys, high-resolution environmental reanalysis, and event-level conflict data, applying multilevel and Bayesian spatiotemporal models. Where data licences permit, replication code accompanies published work, and methodological tools are released as documented repositories.

Climate & Population Health

Heat exposure, healthcare access, and maternal–perinatal outcomes in Ghana

Climate change is increasingly recognised as a demographic question. Rising temperatures, more frequent heat extremes, and shifting precipitation patterns have measurable consequences for fertility, pregnancy outcomes, and infant survival — particularly in low-income tropical settings where baseline thermal stress is already high and health-system capacity to mitigate exposure is constrained. Yet the demographic literature has only recently begun to integrate the high-resolution climate data necessary to quantify these relationships precisely.

My dissertation links ERA5-HEAT reanalysis data with the Ghana Demographic and Health Surveys (2003–2022) to estimate the effects of extreme heat exposure on pregnancy outcomes and infant survival. Methodologically, the work moves away from absolute heat thresholds in favour of location-specific percentile thresholds — defining exposure relative to each cluster’s historical distribution — on the grounds that vulnerability is context-dependent and absolute thresholds misrepresent local risk. The analytical framework draws on Bayesian spatiotemporal modelling, distributed lag non-linear models, and a target-trial emulation approach to causal inference.

A second strand of this theme examines how healthcare access mediates the relationship between heat exposure and infant mortality. The hypothesis is that the demographic consequences of climate stress are unevenly distributed not only by exposure but by the capacity of local health systems to absorb that stress. Forthcoming work extends the framework beyond Ghana to a multi-country comparison of climate-vulnerable settings in sub-Saharan Africa, and connects the empirical findings to the policy infrastructure emerging from the COP30 Belém Health Action Plan.

Health Inequalities

Ethnic and geographic disparities in survival and health-service access

National-level summary statistics are an indispensable tool for population health surveillance, yet they routinely conceal the heterogeneity that is most consequential for policy. A national under-five mortality rate, for example, can mask differences across ethnic groups, regions, and language communities that are an order of magnitude larger than year-to-year change in the national figure. The methodological and substantive question is how to characterise this heterogeneity rigorously, and how to distinguish disparities that are properly understood as ethnic from those that are an artefact of geographic distribution.

My recent article in SSM-Population Health examines ethnic disparities in under-five mortality across fourteen sub-Saharan African countries. Using survey-weighted multilevel models with sensitivity tests for the role of administrative geography, the analysis finds that ethnic disparities are partially — but unevenly — mediated by geographic location. The implication is that disparity statistics are sensitive to the level of geographic adjustment, and that surveillance systems reporting ethnic gaps without geographic decomposition will systematically misstate the relative contribution of ethnic stratification to overall inequality.

Related work, much of it co-authored, examines disparities in health-service access — skilled birth attendance, antenatal care utilisation, intermittent preventive treatment uptake, and home-birth practices — across a range of sub-Saharan African contexts. A common methodological thread is the use of multilevel models to separate individual, household, community, and country-level contributions to observed disparities. Future work will extend the inequality framework to fertility transitions and to cross-country comparisons of educational attainment.

Spatial Demography

Methods, computational infrastructure, and reproducible pipelines

The substantive themes above depend on a methodological foundation: the rigorous linkage of population surveys with environmental, conflict, and administrative data, and the use of models that respect the spatial and hierarchical structure of those data. This third theme treats methodology as a research output in its own right rather than purely as a means to substantive ends. Tools developed in the course of dissertation and collaborative work are released as documented repositories, with the goal of reducing the start-up cost for other researchers working on similar questions.

Methodological work in progress includes a generalisable pipeline for linking Demographic and Health Survey clusters with high-resolution heat-stress reanalysis data (ERA5-HEAT), a target-trial emulation framework for causal inference under non-randomised exposure in observational survey data, and Bayesian spatiotemporal models implemented in R-INLA using the BYM2 specification. A separate methodological strand addresses computational infrastructure: a recent project on conflict and child nutrition in Nigeria required fitting thousands of fixed-effects models in parallel on Windows, motivating the development of a disk-based parallel-worker architecture using independent Rscript processes coordinated through disk files. The utility is generalisable beyond the original use case.

A commitment to reproducibility runs through this theme. Replication code accompanies published work where data licences permit. Where the underlying data are restricted — as is the case for DHS individual records, the SCAD conflict database, and the MLSFH cohort — pipelines are written so that they operate against user-supplied extracts, with the data-access process documented separately.
